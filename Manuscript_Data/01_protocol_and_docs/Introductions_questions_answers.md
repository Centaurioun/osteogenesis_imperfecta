Please provide 2-4 compact manuscript-usable sentences with in-text citations and clearly distinguish well-established points from more tentative ones.

**Answer 1:**

Although osteogenesis imperfecta (OI) is primarily characterized by skeletal fragility and low bone mass, it is well established that it functions as a systemic connective tissue disorder with clinically significant oro-dental and craniofacial manifestations (Forlino & Marini, 2016). The most universally recognized and consistently documented oral phenotype is dentinogenesis imperfecta (DI), a condition driven by type I collagen defects that leads to pronounced tooth discoloration, pulpal obliteration, and severe dental attrition (Malmgren & Norgren, 2002). Beyond these definitive findings, accumulating but phenotypically variable evidence suggests that individuals with OI also face a heightened risk for a broader spectrum of developmental anomalies—such as tooth agenesis, taurodontism, and Class III malocclusions—highlighting a complex and still-emerging relationship between specific genetic variants and global craniofacial development (Andersson et al., 2017; Waltimo-Siren et al., 2005).

* **Dentinogenesis Imperfecta (DI):** OI'nın en belirgin dental manifestasyonudur. Klinik olarak opalesan (gri, mavi, sarı-kahverengi) renklenme; radyografik olarak bulböz kuron, belirgin servikal daralma, kısa kökler ve pulpa odasının erken obliterasyonu ile karakterizedir. The evidence regarding the caries burden in individuals with Osteogenesis Imperfecta (OI) is decidedly **mixed and sparse**, characterized by conflicting epidemiological data and a reliance on studies with significant methodological heterogeneity.

**Evidence Status: OI-Specific vs. Broader Indirect Evidence**
Direct clinical evidence on whether OI inherently increases susceptibility to dental caries yields contradictory conclusions. Some epidemiological studies report that the overall caries prevalence in the OI population does not significantly differ from the baseline prevalence of the general healthy population, suggesting that the collagen defect itself does not inherently elevate caries risk (Nguyen et al., 2021); (Ventura et al., 2024). Conversely, other recent case-control analyses indicate a distinct vulnerability; for instance, pediatric patients with OI have been shown to exhibit significantly higher decayed, missing, and filled teeth (dmft/DMFT) scores compared to age- and gender-matched healthy peers (Defabianis et al., 2024). Furthermore, an evaluation of a Turkish cohort revealed "high and very high" DMFT values across all age groups of OI patients according to World Health Organization diagnostic criteria (Demir et al., 2025).

Rather than a direct systemic vulnerability caused by OI, the literature identifies a strong OI-specific internal predictor: the co-occurrence of Dentinogenesis Imperfecta (DI). A large cross-sectional multicenter study found that the presence of DI acts as a significant independent predictor of increased caries experience, effectively doubling the odds (OR 2.43) when controlling for age and other demographic factors (Ma et al., 2019).

Indirectly, the structural anomalies associated with DI—such as an altered dentino-enamel junction and rapid enamel shedding—theoretically expose the underlying structurally defective dentin to bacterial invasion and rapid degradation (Ventura et al., 2024). However, an older, contrasting mechanistic hypothesis proposed that the rapid attrition and early obliteration of dentinal tubules seen in DI might actually impede the deep progression of carious lesions, illustrating the theoretical ambiguity that persists in the broader dental literature (Ma et al., 2019).

**Pediatric Applicability**
The available literature is predominantly based on pediatric and adolescent cohorts. In the primary dentition, DI expression is generally more severe, and the associated enamel fracturing heavily compromises tooth integrity (Nguyen et al., 2021). While the prevalence of caries in the primary teeth of children with OI is markedly high (e.g., 86% in a Vietnamese cohort), researchers caution that this often mirrors the high early childhood caries rates of the regional background population rather than an isolated disease effect (Nguyen et al., 2021). Furthermore, reduced stimulated salivary flow rates and compromised oral hygiene—often due to a prioritization of severe orthopedic and medical care over dental maintenance—frequently exacerbate the pediatric caries burden in this population (Defabianis et al., 2024).

**Methodological Caveats (For a Cautious Introduction)**
To maintain a scientifically rigorous tone in a manuscript Introduction, claims regarding the caries burden in OI should be tempered by acknowledging the following methodological limitations in the current evidence base:
1. **Index Distortions (Missing Teeth Bias):** Traditional DMFT/dmft indices are inherently skewed in the OI population due to high rates of tooth agenesis, impactions, and extractions secondary to severe DI breakdown or orthodontic needs rather than strictly carious decay. Consequently, some researchers advocate for mathematically adjusted DFT indices to prevent the overestimation of the caries experience, making direct comparisons with standard literature difficult (Ma et al., 2019); (Elfituri et al., 2024).
2. **Lack of Standardized Control Groups:** Many studies lack healthy, matched control groups and fail to adjust for critical confounders such as regional socioeconomic status, dietary habits, and background caries prevalence (Defabianis et al., 2024).
3. **Phenotypic Heterogeneity:** The pooling of patients with diverse OI clinical types (Sillence I through V), varying severities of DI, and differing bisphosphonate treatment histories obscures the exact etiology of the caries burden, preventing definitive genotype-phenotype correlations regarding decay susceptibility (Demir et al., 2025).

**Answer 1:**

The evidence regarding the caries burden in individuals with osteogenesis imperfecta (OI) is sparse and somewhat mixed (Prado et al., 2019).

Much of the available literature provides indirect evidence by grouping children with OI alongside those with other rare skeletal disorders, such as mucopolysaccharidoses (MPS), to achieve adequate statistical power (Prado et al., 2019; Vettore et al., 2020). These combined cohort studies demonstrate that individuals with rare skeletal diseases are 2.92 times more likely to have dental caries than healthy controls, highlighting a pathway where physical limitations and poor oral hygiene directly link the genetic condition to an increased caries risk (Prado et al., 2019; Vettore et al., 2020). Conversely, OI-specific clinical evaluations present mixed conclusions (Marulanda et al., 2024; Sanches et al., 2006). While dentinogenesis imperfecta (DI) has been identified as an independent risk factor for developing dental caries in individuals with OI, other evidence suggests that teeth affected by OI are not intrinsically more susceptible to caries than normal teeth (Marulanda et al., 2024; Sanches et al., 2006). Instead, high caries activity in these patients is more likely the result of inappropriate oral hygiene habits rather than the structural type I collagen defect itself (Sanches et al., 2006).

This evidence is highly applicable to pediatric populations (Prado et al., 2019; Teixeira et al., 2021; Vettore et al., 2020). The primary studies assessing caries vulnerability, oral hygiene impacts, and health disparities specifically recruited cohorts of children and adolescents, such as a study with a mean age of 10.34 years and another focusing on children and adolescents with an average age of 7.2 to 8.1 years (Prado et al., 2019; Teixeira et al., 2021; Vettore et al., 2020).

Several methodological caveats necessitate cautious claims in introductory contexts (Prado et al., 2019; Vettore et al., 2020). First, the pooling of OI patients with those suffering from MPS limits the ability to isolate the specific caries risk strictly attributable to the OI phenotype (Prado et al., 2019; Vettore et al., 2020). Second, the reliance on cross-sectional study designs impedes the establishment of a definitive causal relationship between the OI disease process and the development of dental caries (Prado et al., 2019; Teixeira et al., 2021). Third, these analyses frequently fail to investigate or adjust for unmeasured confounding behavioral and environmental risk factors, such as a cariogenic diet and the use of fluorides (Vettore et al., 2020). Finally, caries vulnerability is heavily confounded by psychosocial and care barriers; the stressful routine of frequent hospitalizations, combined with parental fear of causing jaw fractures during tooth brushing or dental visits, often leads to the neglect of daily oral hygiene and preventive dental care (Prado et al., 2019; Sanches et al., 2006; Teixeira et al., 2021).

1. **Index Distortions (Missing Teeth Bias):** Traditional DMFT/dmft indices are inherently skewed in the OI population due to high rates of tooth agenesis, impactions, and extractions secondary to severe DI breakdown or orthodontic needs rather than strictly carious decay. ### Defensible Limitations of Prior Studies on Oro-Dental Findings in OI

#### 1. Small Sample Sizes and Cohort Composition Issues
A primary limitation in the existing literature is the restricted sample size inherent to studying a rare disease like osteogenesis imperfecta (OI), which significantly affects statistical power and the generalizability of findings (Etik Kurul Basvuru, n.d.). Many clinical reports describing hereditary dentin defects rely on data from merely one or two families, making broad phenotypic comparisons challenging (Li et al., 2017). Furthermore, cohort compositions are frequently confounded by familial clustering; studies that include multiple siblings from the same family risk skewing the prevalence data by overweighting specific, shared genetic traits (Malmgren & Norgren, 2002). Retrospective, single-center study designs further limit the applicability of the results across diverse geographic and ethnic populations (Etik Kurul Basvuru, n.d.).

#### 2. Inconsistent Phenotype Definitions and Diagnostic Criteria
There is significant variability in the diagnostic criteria utilized across studies to define dentinogenesis imperfecta (DI) and other dental anomalies, which complicates cross-study comparisons (Etik Kurul Basvuru, n.d.). A critical methodological flaw is the over-reliance on clinical visual inspection (tooth discoloration) to diagnose DI. Clinical appearance is an unreliable indicator of underlying dentin health, as teeth appearing normal in color frequently exhibit "subclinical" DI characterized by severe histologic and radiographic aberrations (Malmgren & Lindskog, 2003). Microscopic dentin defects have been documented in up to 19% of OI patients who display no overt clinical signs of DI (Andersson et al., 2017).

#### 3. Limited Genetic Stratification
Historically, the literature has correlated oro-dental findings primarily with the clinical Sillence classification (Types I-IV) rather than analyzing the precise underlying molecular pathology (Literature Review 1, n.d.). Although genetic variants are increasingly recognized as more accurate predictors of dental phenotypes than clinical disease severity, robust genotype-phenotype correlation studies remain elusive and are often limited by small cohort sizes (Liu et al., 2017). Many prior studies fail to systematically stratify patients based on the nature of the collagen defect (e.g., quantitative haploinsufficiency versus qualitative structural defects) or the specific location of the mutation (e.g., *COL1A1* vs. *COL1A2*), leaving significant gaps in understanding the genetic drivers of the dental phenotype (Andersson et al., 2017).

#### 4. Narrow Outcome Scope and Integration Difficulties
The literature is highly fragmented, with most research focusing on isolated oral endpoints. Studies frequently evaluate malocclusion, tooth agenesis, or DI independently, rather than providing a holistic, integrated assessment of the entire oro-dental and craniofacial phenotype within the same cohort. There is a notable deficiency in studies utilizing standardized, comprehensive radiographic analyses to concurrently evaluate interrelated anomalies such as taurodontism, pulp chamber obliteration, and agenesis patterns (Literature Review 1, n.d.). This narrow scope limits the ability to map complex interactions between multiple dental anomalies and specific genetic mutations.

#### 5. Age-Related Variables and Dentition Discrepancies
Oro-dental manifestations in OI exhibit pronounced temporal and intra-oral variability, yet many studies fail to account for these age-related dynamics. ### Methodological Limitations in Current OI Dental Literature

While the association between osteogenesis imperfecta (OI) and oro-dental anomalies is well documented, the current understanding of this relationship is hindered by several persistent methodological limitations in the literature.

**Small Sample Sizes and Cohort Composition Issues**
Because OI is a rare disease, the vast majority of dental studies rely on small, convenience samples that lack statistical power and broad representativeness (Broutin et al., 2024); (Pantoja et al., 2024); (Prado et al., 2023). Furthermore, these cohorts frequently lack healthy, age- and sex-matched control groups, which precludes robust comparative analyses to accurately quantify the true excess risk of specific dental anomalies in the OI population (Broutin et al., 2024); (Defabianis et al., 2024). Additionally, many studies pool data across wide age ranges (merging primary, mixed, and permanent dentitions) and fail to control for significant clinical confounders, most notably the duration and early initiation of intravenous bisphosphonate therapy, which independently alters tooth eruption and root morphology (Ventura et al., 2024).

**Inconsistent Phenotype Definitions**
The literature suffers from a severe lack of standardized diagnostic criteria and uniform outcome measures for evaluating the dental phenotype (Blokland et al., 2024). For instance, the diagnosis of dentinogenesis imperfecta (DI) is highly inconsistent across studies; some authors rely exclusively on clinical tooth discoloration, whereas others require specific radiographic features or histological confirmation (Ventura et al., 2024). Similarly, there is a lack of validated, universally accepted definitions for grading the severity of other frequent radiographic findings, such as pulp obliteration, cervical constriction, and short roots (Thuesen et al., 2018).

**Limited Genetic Stratification**
Historically, dental assessments in OI have been stratified almost exclusively based on the clinical Sillence classification (Types I–IV) rather than specific molecular diagnoses (Demir et al., 2025). Because the clinical classification groups together patients with vastly different genetic mutations, drawing definitive genotype-dental phenotype correlations remains challenging. Only a limited number of recent studies have attempted to correlate dental anomalies with specific pathogenic variants (e.g., qualitative versus quantitative *COL1A1/COL1A2* defects), and data regarding the dental impact of rare, recessively inherited non-collagenous mutations are notably scarce and derived mostly from isolated case reports (Demir et al., 2025); (Taqi et al., 2021).

**Narrow Outcome Scope and Lack of Endpoint Integration**
Finally, prior research has largely maintained a narrow outcome scope, typically isolating single variables—such as the presence of DI, the prevalence of malocclusion, or patient-reported quality of life—rather than exploring the interplay between these factors. There is a distinct paucity of studies capable of comprehensively integrating macroscopic anomalies, radiographic findings (e.g., taurodontism, agenesis, impaction), functional parameters, and oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL) within the same well-characterized cohort (Blokland et al., 2024); (Ventura et al., 2024).

**1. Claim to Soften: "OI intrinsically increases the biological susceptibility to dental caries."**
* **Why it is tempting:** Patients with OI frequently present with poor oral health, making it easy to assume that defective type I collagen directly causes enamel and dentin to decay faster.
* **Why it should be softened:** The evidence is mixed and largely indirect. Many studies pool OI patients with individuals suffering from other rare genetic diseases (like mucopolysaccharidoses) to achieve statistical power, masking OI-specific risks (Prado et al., 2019); (Vettore et al., 2020). Furthermore, while dentinogenesis imperfecta (DI) is a risk factor, the high caries burden in OI is heavily confounded by psychosocial barriers—such as poor oral hygiene due to parental fear of causing jaw fractures during brushing, and limited access to specialized dental care—rather than the structural collagen defect alone (Sanches et al., 2006); (Teixeira et al., 2021).

**2. Claim to Soften: "The prevalence of dentinogenesis imperfecta (DI) in OI is exactly [X]%."**
* **Why it is tempting:** Providing a definitive prevalence rate establishes the magnitude of the problem quickly.
* **Why it should be softened:** Literature shows extreme variance, with reported DI prevalences fluctuating wildly from 8% to 100% (Taqi et al., 2021). This is due to severe methodological limitations across studies, including the pooling of primary and permanent dentitions (which express DI differently), failure to assess "subclinical" radiographic/histological DI in teeth that appear clinically normal in color, and the inclusion of multiple siblings from the same family, which skews genetic weighting (Malmgren & Norgren, 2002); (Malmgren & Lindskog, 2003).

**3. Claim to Soften: "Specific dental phenotypes can be reliably predicted for all genetic mutations causing OI."**
* **Why it is tempting:** Genotype-phenotype correlations are highly sought after to predict disease progression and establish treatment protocols.
* **Why it should be softened:** While there is strong evidence linking qualitative/structural *COL1A1* and *COL1A2* mutations to severe DI and oligodontia (Andersson et al., 2017); (Malmgren et al., 2017), the evidence regarding rare, non-collagenous (recessive) mutations is sparse and limited to small case series. For example, reports on *IFITM5* (OI Type V) or *FKBP10* (OI Type XI) show highly variable or conflicting findings regarding missing teeth and subclinical DI, meaning broad genotype-phenotype generalizations should not be applied to non-classical OI types (Retrouvey et al., 2019); (Chetty et al., 2019); (Marulanda et al., 2024).

**4. Claim to Soften: "Bisphosphonate therapy places pediatric OI patients at high risk for Medication-Related Osteonecrosis of the Jaw (MRONJ)."**
* **Why it is tempting:** Intravenous bisphosphonates are a well-documented cause of MRONJ in adults (particularly oncology patients), and bisphosphonates are the gold standard for pediatric OI treatment.
* **Why it should be softened:** The translation of adult oncology risks to the pediatric OI population lacks direct evidence. Systematic reviews have demonstrated that confirmed cases of MRONJ in children and adolescents with OI following dental extractions or surgery are exceedingly rare or non-existent in the literature (Chahine et al., 2008); (Hennedige et al., 2013). While delayed healing or delayed tooth eruption is consistently reported, "osteonecrosis" should not be presented as an established pediatric complication.

### 1. The Definitive Prevalence of Dentinogenesis Imperfecta (DI)

* **The Tempting Claim:** The structural defects of DI make OI patients highly susceptible to severe dental caries.

* **The Tempting Claim:** Specific genetic mutations in *COL1A1* or *COL1A2* directly dictate the presence of specific morphological anomalies like malocclusion, tooth agenesis, or taurodontism.

The precise mechanistic pathways translating mutant genotypes into morphological defects like Class III malocclusion or agenesis are not fully elucidated, making direct predictive claims premature.

### 4. High MRONJ Risk as a Primary Barrier in Pediatric Dental Care
* **The Tempting Claim:** Bisphosphonate-related osteonecrosis of the jaw (MRONJ) is a major, imminent risk for pediatric OI patients undergoing dental treatments.

**Answer 3:**

Based on a rigorous synthesis of the provided literature, here is a prioritized list of claims that are tempting to include in an Introduction but must be softened or carefully contextualized. The evidence supporting these specific claims is currently mixed, sparse, or affected by methodological limitations.

**1. Claim: Osteogenesis imperfecta inherently increases the direct susceptibility to dental caries.**
* **Why to soften:** The evidence regarding caries prevalence in OI is notably mixed and methodologically limited. While some epidemiological cohorts present high decayed, missing, and filled teeth (DMFT) scores, other studies report that the overall caries prevalence in patients with OI is not significantly different from the general population (Nguyen et al., 2021); (Ventura et al., 2024). Rather than a direct systemic vulnerability caused by the disease itself, an increased caries risk is primarily an indirect consequence of Dentinogenesis Imperfecta (DI), which causes rapid enamel shedding and exposes the underlying dentin to bacterial degradation (Ma et al., 2019). Furthermore, the use of standard DMFT/dmft indices in OI cohorts is often methodologically skewed by the inherently high rates of tooth agenesis, impactions, and extractions secondary to severe DI, thereby overestimating true carious decay (Ma et al., 2019).

**2. Claim: Specific non-collagenous (recessive) gene variants yield predictable, clearly defined dental phenotypes.**
* **Why to soften:** While the relationship between classic qualitative type I collagen defects (*COL1A1/COL1A2*) and the presence of severe DI is robustly supported, the evidence correlating specific oro-dental manifestations with rare, recessively inherited non-collagenous gene variants (e.g., *FKBP10*, *P3H1*, *SERPINF1*) remains sparse and sometimes contradictory (Chetty et al., 2021); (Demir et al., 2025). Claims regarding these rare variants should be cautious, as current literature indicates marked intrafamilial and interfamilial phenotypic variability, meaning clinical expressivity can differ significantly even among individuals carrying the exact same pathogenic variant (Chetty et al., 2021).

**3. Claim: Patients with mild OI who have normal-looking teeth are completely free of dentin defects.**
* **Why to soften:** It is tempting to categorize OI patients into strict "DI-positive" and "DI-negative" groups based on clinical appearance, but epidemiological studies lack standardized diagnostic criteria for DI (Ventura et al., 2024). Morphological and histological evaluations demonstrate that subclinical ultrastructural dentin anomalies frequently exist in the teeth of individuals with OI, even when macroscopic discoloration and classic radiographic signs of DI are absent (Martin-Vacas et al., 2022).

**4. Claim: Eruption delays, impactions, and root dysmorphologies are purely natural manifestations of the OI disease process.**
* **Why to soften:** Descriptions of the "natural" oro-dental phenotype in contemporary OI populations are heavily confounded by the early and widespread use of antiresorptive therapies. Because tooth eruption and root formation depend on osteoclast-mediated bone resorption, early-onset bisphosphonate (BP) treatment independently induces iatrogenic eruption delays, increased impaction rates, and short, blunted dental roots (Del Rio Cantero et al., 2024); (Malmgren et al., 2021); (Taqi et al., 2021). Therefore, phenotypic claims about eruption and root form must acknowledge the confounding role of BP therapy.
